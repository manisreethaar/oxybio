Karuppu Kavuni: The GI-Tagged Black Rice We’re Helping Keep Alive

Karuppu Kavuni is not just a co-fermentation substrate for us. It is a heirloom grain with a conservation story that deserves to be told on its own terms.

When we talk about the grain base behind our beverage platform, Ragi (finger millet) usually gets top billing — it is widely known, well-studied, and easy to explain. Karuppu Kavuni, the black rice variety we use alongside it, gets mentioned far less. We want to fix that, because its story is arguably more interesting.

What Makes Karuppu Kavuni Different

Karuppu Kavuni is an heirloom black rice variety traditionally cultivated in parts of Tamil Nadu. It carries a Geographical Indication (GI) tag — a legal recognition that ties the grain's identity to the specific region and farming communities that have cultivated it for generations. GI tags exist precisely because varieties like this one are vulnerable: they yield less per acre than commodity rice, they take longer to grow, and without deliberate demand, farmers have every economic incentive to switch to higher-yield, lower-effort crops.

Nutritionally, Karuppu Kavuni is distinguished by its anthocyanin content — the same class of pigment compounds responsible for the colour of blueberries and purple cabbage — along with a denser dietary fibre profile than typical polished rice. In our process, it serves as a co-fermentation substrate alongside Ragi, contributing natural colour, antioxidant activity, and a more complex fermentable carbohydrate profile than either grain would provide alone.

Why Sourcing Choices Matter

Every kilogram of Karuppu Kavuni we source for fermentation R&D is a kilogram of demand for a heirloom variety that is at real risk of being crowded out by commodity rice. We do not think this makes us a conservation organisation — we are a biotech company solving a beverage problem — but we do think sourcing decisions like this one are worth being transparent about.

A Note on What We Don't Publish

We get asked, reasonably often, exactly how much Karuppu Kavuni goes into a given batch relative to Ragi, and exactly how the two substrates are combined and processed. We do not publish that level of process detail publicly — our specific substrate ratios and fermentation sequencing are part of the proprietary process work we are protecting ahead of patent filing. What we are happy to share is the ingredient story itself: why we chose this grain, what it nutritionally contributes, and why its conservation matters independently of our commercial interest in it.

The Bigger Picture

India has an enormous, underused catalogue of heirloom grains and millets, many of them GI-tagged, many of them at risk of disappearing from active cultivation simply because there is no commercial pull keeping them in the ground. We think there is a genuine opportunity — beyond our own product roadmap — for functional food companies in India to build demand-side pressure that keeps varieties like Karuppu Kavuni economically viable for the farmers who grow them.

We will keep telling this story as our sourcing relationships develop, and we will be transparent about where our grain comes from as we scale beyond Phase 0 R&D volumes.
