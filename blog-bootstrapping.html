Inside DETI@ACE TBI: what R&D looks like at Phase 0

No glossy labs. No manufactured optimism. An honest account of running laboratory research inside a university incubator, from early failures to where we are now.

Every startup that touches biotech eventually has to answer the same uncomfortable question: where, exactly, is the science happening? For some, the answer is rented clean-room space in a pharmaceutical hub. For us, at this stage, the answer is DETI@ACE TBI at Adhiyamaan College of Engineering, Hosur.

I want to be honest about what that means in practice, because the usual biotech startup narrative tends to polish early-stage research into something it is not.

What Phase 0 looks like

Phase 0, in our context, means we do not yet have a validated, reproducible process. We have a hypothesis, and we are running systematic experiments to find out whether it holds.

Our early runs did not all work. Some failed outright; others taught us things we had not accounted for. Each run costs money, time and material.

Status, April 2026. The work has progressed far enough for instrumental analysis to begin. Formal validation is pending, and we will publish results when they are confirmed.

Why we chose a TBI over a contract manufacturer

The first instinct when building a food product is to go straight to a contract manufacturer. They have equipment, process engineers and regulatory compliance. But every contract manufacturer we spoke to in the early phase gave the same advice: simplify the formula and use cheaper carriers. The economics of their business do not accommodate low-volume, slow, innovation-led formulation work.

DETI@ACE TBI understood the brief immediately. We were not pitching a consumer product; we were pitching a primary research programme on indigenous Indian grains. The TBI gave us laboratory access, mentorship and institutional credibility, as part of India's growing deep-tech incubation ecosystem.

If you want to build a cheap product, there are a thousand factories ready to help you. If you want to build something real, you have to own the research environment first.

The honest realities of incubation

Incubation is not glamorous. Laboratory hours are shared. Equipment bookings sometimes clash with other researchers' needs. There are days when a run produces nothing useful and you start again. The data is messy and inconclusive in ways no pitch deck acknowledges.

Those constraints are also what make the output credible. Every run we document, every failure, every instrumental reading builds an evidence trail. When we have a validated formula, it will be the result of a systematic process, not of a marketing team deciding what to print on a label.

What comes next

Once the first results are formally validated, the next phase is sensory work: what the base tastes, smells and feels like, and whether it can become something people want to drink every day. That problem is harder than it sounds, and we will write about it when we get there.

We are building in public because the Indian functional food market deserves to see what honest, evidence-led product development requires. If that transparency costs us some early polish, it is a cost we are willing to pay.
